Most training programmes are organised around a taxonomy that does not exist in the body.

Chest day. Back day. Leg day. Shoulder day. The split feels logical — it names the tissue you intend to stress, groups similar exercises, and creates clear days off for each body part. The problem is that the body does not organise its output this way. When you squat, you are not issuing a command to your legs. You are executing a motor pattern that recruits an entire kinetic chain — glutes, quads, hamstrings, spinal erectors, core, upper back — in a coordinated sequence determined by the pattern's mechanical demands.

A muscle-group split is a filing system imposed on top of a reality that refuses to cooperate with it. The body does not know it is doing chest day. It knows it is pressing.

---

## The seven patterns

The engine organises training around seven fundamental movement patterns. These are not categories invented by the system — they represent the irreducible set of human movement functions that, when trained systematically, produce complete neuromuscular development.

**Squat** — the bilateral knee-dominant lower body pattern. Goblet squat, back squat, front squat, box squat. The defining characteristic: the hips and knees flex and extend simultaneously under load, with the torso remaining relatively upright.

**Hinge** — the hip-dominant posterior chain pattern. Deadlift, Romanian deadlift, trap bar deadlift, kettlebell swing. The defining characteristic: the hips flex while the knees remain relatively fixed, driving load into the posterior chain — glutes, hamstrings, spinal erectors.

**Push — horizontal and vertical** — the pressing patterns. Horizontal: bench press, push-up, dumbbell press. Vertical: overhead press, landmine press, dumbbell shoulder press. Treated as two sub-patterns because they train different portions of the pressing chain and create different shoulder joint demands.

**Pull — horizontal and vertical** — the pulling patterns. Horizontal: barbell row, cable row, dumbbell row, face pull. Vertical: pull-up, lat pull-down, chin-up. Same logic as push: the two sub-patterns are not interchangeable. Vertical pulling develops the lats primarily; horizontal pulling develops the mid-back and rear deltoid. Both are required every week.

**Rotation and anti-rotation** — the core as a force-transfer mechanism. Pallof press, cable chop, medicine ball rotational throw, landmine rotation. This pattern addresses a function the other six do not: the ability to generate and resist rotational force. Every sport and most functional movements demand it. Most programmes omit it entirely.

**Carry and locomotion** — structural resilience under time and distance. Farmer's carry, suitcase carry, single-arm overhead carry. The carry pattern is the most underrated of the seven. It trains the nervous system under sustained load, develops grip and shoulder stability, builds anti-lateral-flexion strength, and conditions the structural tissues in a way that brief, high-intensity work does not.

**Jump** — the expression of lower-body power in the vertical or horizontal plane. Box jump, broad jump, vertical jump, depth jump. Power is force times velocity. The jump pattern is the only one in the seven that specifically trains the high-velocity end of that equation. Remove it from training and you train strength without its sporting expression.

## Why coverage matters

Every pattern corresponds to a distinct motor programme in the nervous system. A motor programme is not just a list of muscles — it is a coordinated sequence of neural commands, joint angles, force vectors, and timing relationships that the nervous system has learned to produce reliably.

Training a pattern reinforces its motor programme. Neglecting a pattern allows it to decay. The specificity principle — the body adapts to the specific demands placed on it — works in both directions.

The consequence: an athlete who trains squat, hinge, horizontal push, and horizontal pull has a complete lower body and partial upper body programme. Their vertical pull motor programme (lats, scapular retractors, the stabilisers that control pulling overhead) is undertrained. Their carry and rotation patterns are likely absent. The joints and tissues involved in those patterns are weaker, less stable, and more vulnerable.

This is not a minor gap. The shoulder girdle's stability under overhead load depends on the vertical pull pattern. The spine's ability to transfer force laterally depends on the anti-rotation pattern. The body's structural resilience under sustained load depends on carries.

A programme that does not cover all seven patterns is not a complete programme. It is a partial programme that happens to look complete because it names muscles rather than functions.

## What splits actually look like through a pattern lens

Take a standard four-day push/pull/legs/shoulders split and map it to the seven patterns:

| Day | Split label | Patterns covered |
|-----|-------------|-----------------|
| Monday | Push | Horizontal push (partial vertical push) |
| Tuesday | Pull | Horizontal pull, vertical pull |
| Thursday | Legs | Squat, hinge |
| Friday | Shoulders | Vertical push (again) |

The pattern gaps: rotation/anti-rotation is absent. Carry is absent. Jump is absent. Horizontal push gets a full day; carries and rotation, which arguably produce more athletic carryover than an extra horizontal press day, get nothing.

This is the norm, not the exception. The push/pull/legs structure emerged from bodybuilding, where the goal is hypertrophy in specific muscle groups. Applied to performance training — where the goals are strength, work capacity, movement quality, and resilience — it produces systematic gaps that compound over time.

## How the engine enforces coverage

The engine contains an explicit weekly coverage requirement. Every programme generated must include sessions that collectively hit all seven patterns within the training week. This is not a recommendation — it is a hard rule applied before the LLM touches the programme.

The deterministic decision engine resolves this before model generation begins: given the client's schedule (how many days per week, which days), it assigns patterns to sessions and verifies coverage before proceeding. If the schedule cannot accommodate all seven patterns, the engine attempts to combine patterns in single sessions before escalating to a constraint error.

The practical effect: no programme generated by the system will omit rotation, carry, or jump in favour of a fifth pressing session. The coverage constraint makes the omission impossible.

## The interplay between patterns

The patterns are not independent. They interact, and the quality of execution in one affects the capacity in others.

The hinge and carry patterns share the posterior chain demand. Improving the hinge — specifically the ability to generate hip extension force — transfers directly to carry capacity, because the loaded carry demands sustained hip-extension and spinal-stability output. Train the hinge and the carry gets stronger even when carries are not progressed.

The rotation and pull patterns share the scapular and thoracic spine demand. The ability to produce rotation through the thorax depends on the same scapular stability that pulls train. Neglect pulling and rotation quality degrades.

The jump and squat patterns share the knee-dominant lower body chain, but the jump pattern specifically trains the rate of force development — how fast force is produced, not just how much. A well-developed squat with no jump training produces high peak force output and low rate of force development. In athletic contexts, rate of force development frequently matters more than peak force: the first step, the jump, the change of direction all happen too fast for peak force to be expressed. The jump pattern is how you convert squat strength into athletic power.

## The weekly minimum

The engine's coverage requirement does not demand equal volume for each pattern — it demands presence. Every pattern must appear in the week's programme. From there, the volume allocation follows the client's goals and the objective hierarchy.

A programme oriented toward strength will allocate more volume to squat and hinge. A programme with a power emphasis will weight jump and carry more heavily. A programme for a rotational sport athlete will allocate more rotation volume than a programme for a purely linear sport.

What none of these programmes will do is omit a pattern entirely. The minimum is presence. The distribution beyond that is objective-driven.

## Applying this

Look at your last training week. Map each session to the seven patterns. Mark which ones appeared and which ones were absent.

If you are running a standard muscle-group split, you will almost certainly find rotation, carry, and jump missing. If you are running a push/pull/legs structure, you may find vertical push is overtrained relative to vertical pull. If you are doing purely gym-based training, jump may not have appeared in months.

The gaps are not random — they are systematic, determined by the structure of the split. The split creates them by design.

The correction is not to add exercises randomly. It is to organise the training week around pattern coverage first, then allocate volume to individual exercises. Seven patterns. One week. Everything else follows from that.
