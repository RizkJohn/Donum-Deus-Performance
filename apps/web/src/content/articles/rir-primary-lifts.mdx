The case for training to failure sounds intuitive: maximum effort produces maximum stimulus. If you stop short of failure, you are leaving adaptation on the table. Push harder, grow more.

This argument is wrong in a specific way that becomes more consequential as training load increases.

Failure on a primary compound lift — a squat, a deadlift, a press — is not a training target. It is a system event. It taxes the nervous system, disrupts motor patterns, elevates injury risk, and generates fatigue that has no proportional benefit in the adaptations it produces. The 1–3 Reps in Reserve (RIR) ceiling on primary lifts is not a conservative modification to standard programming. It is the correct ceiling, derived from what the data on proximity-to-failure and motor pattern quality actually shows.

---

## What RIR means

Reps in Reserve is a measure of proximity to concentric failure. An RIR of 1 means you could have completed one more rep before the movement broke down irreversibly. An RIR of 3 means three more reps remained in the tank.

The scale runs from 0 (failure) to approximately 10 (very light, far from failure). The RIR 1–3 window for primary lifts means: you work hard enough to produce a meaningful training stimulus, and stop before the point at which the fatigue-to-signal ratio tips unfavourably.

This is not subjective. Research on velocity-based training — which measures bar speed to infer proximity to failure — has consistently shown that the last 1–2 reps before failure produce disproportionately more fatigue than the reps at RIR 2–3, without producing proportionately more hypertrophy or strength stimulus. The marginal gain from those final reps is small. The marginal fatigue cost is high.

---

## The two types of fatigue

To understand why RIR matters on primaries specifically, you need to distinguish between the two distinct fatigue accounts that training draws from.

**Peripheral fatigue** is local to the muscle. It accumulates during any set that takes a muscle to or near its capacity. It recovers relatively quickly — within 24–72 hours depending on the volume and muscle group — and it responds to nutrition, sleep, and rest days. Peripheral fatigue from a high-volume accessory circuit will resolve before the next session in most cases.

**Central fatigue** — CNS fatigue — is different. It accumulates from high-effort motor unit recruitment. The neural drive required to move a heavy barbell near maximal load is fundamentally different from the neural drive required to complete a high-rep accessory set. The heavy primary lift recruits more motor units, demands greater neural coordination, and stresses the neuromuscular junction more deeply. This fatigue resolves more slowly — 48 to 72 hours minimum — and its effects are not limited to the muscles involved in that lift.

Training to failure on a primary lift draws heavily from both accounts simultaneously. You accumulate peripheral fatigue in the target muscles and maximal central fatigue from the neural recruitment required to grind through a failed attempt. The failed rep itself produces almost none of the mechanical tension that drives hypertrophy and strength — you have already passed through the productive range — but it contributes meaningfully to central fatigue.

## Why primaries are different from accessories

This distinction — between primary and accessory lifts — is where many programmes make a category error.

There is a legitimate case for taking accessory movements closer to failure, or even to failure. A cable fly, a leg curl, a face pull: these movements are single-joint or simple-pattern exercises, load is relatively low, and the central fatigue cost is modest. The risk is bounded. The motor pattern is simple enough that fatigue does not substantially compromise form before failure.

A barbell squat, a deadlift, a overhead press: these are multi-joint compound movements performed under high load. As fatigue accumulates within the set, motor pattern quality degrades. The breakdown follows a predictable sequence — the easiest joints to cheat recruit first, the most vulnerable positions emerge as the system looks for the path of least resistance. A failed squat attempt does not look like a perfect squat attempt performed more slowly. It looks like a different lift.

This matters for two reasons. First, the degraded pattern accumulates less of the productive mechanical tension in the intended muscle groups — the stimulus is lower, not higher, because the lift has changed. Second, the degraded pattern under high load is how primary lift injuries happen. The injury does not occur on the first hard rep. It occurs when accumulated fatigue within the set causes a breakdown in the stabilising structures at exactly the moment load is highest.

The 1–3 RIR ceiling exists because it keeps the movement in the range where motor pattern quality is maintained under load. You are training the pattern correctly — reinforcing the motor programme, applying tension to the target tissues in their intended positions — rather than training the degraded version of the pattern that emerges in the last moments before failure.

## What intensity actually means

The word intensity is used loosely in most training discourse. It is worth being precise.

Intensity in its technical sense means percentage of one-rep max (%1RM). An RIR of 2 on a set of 5 at 85% 1RM is high intensity. An RIR of 0 (failure) on a set of 20 at 60% 1RM is low intensity with high effort. These are different things.

The evidence for strength and neural adaptation is substantially more closely linked to intensity — the actual load on the bar relative to your maximum — than to proximity to failure at that load. Heavy, well-executed sets at 80–90% 1RM, stopped at RIR 1–3, produce robust strength stimulus. They do so because the load itself creates the neural recruitment demand. The final grinding reps before failure add effort; they do not add intensity.

This is why the system never prescribes failure on primaries and why the RIR floor is 1. The training stimulus on a primary lift is primarily determined by the weight on the bar and the quality of execution. Getting to failure does not increase intensity — it only increases fatigue and degradation risk.

## Autoregulation and the RIR signal

The 1–3 RIR range is also the basis for autoregulation — the process by which the engine adjusts load within a session based on the athlete's actual performance state on a given day.

When fatigue is high, bar speed drops. A weight that would feel like RIR 3 at full capacity might feel like RIR 1 when sleep was poor and stress is elevated. The RIR signal tells you what the session cost is on any given day, even when the weights are unchanged.

An athlete who rigidly chases a target weight regardless of RIR is either undertrained (adding no stimulus on high-capacity days) or overtrained (grinding through failure on low-capacity days). The RIR target creates a dynamic ceiling: the load that produces RIR 1–3 is the right load on that day, whatever that weight happens to be.

This is how autoregulation produces better outcomes over a training block than fixed percentage prescriptions. The load is not set in advance — it is calibrated to actual capacity. When capacity is high, load goes up. When capacity is low, the set ends earlier at the same weight. The stimulus is preserved. The system is not overdrawn.

## The compound effect over time

A single training session where you take primary lifts to failure is not a catastrophe. The compound effect over a training block is.

An athlete who trains primaries to failure accumulates:

- Higher peak CNS fatigue after each session
- Longer recovery requirements before full capacity returns
- More sessions where the second half of the week is compromised by the CNS debt from the first half
- Progressive degradation of motor pattern quality as accumulated fatigue alters the movement

Over three months, the athlete who trains at RIR 1–3 on primaries and to failure on accessories has executed every primary session at full capacity. Every set was performed with the motor pattern intact. Every rep produced the stimulus it was supposed to produce. The total productive volume is higher, not lower, than the athlete who trained every set to failure — because the athlete training to failure was doing so on compromised sessions.

Intensity, quality, recovery. The 1–3 RIR rule on primaries protects all three.

---

## What this looks like in practice

The practical implication is simple: stop the set when you have 1–3 reps left. Not when the form breaks down. Before the form breaks down.

If you reach RIR 1 sooner than expected on a given day — the bar feels heavier than it should — that is information. Reduce the weight to hit the target rep count at RIR 2–3. The session prescription is the work quality, not the number at the top of the programme. The number is an estimate of what will produce the right stimulus on an average day. Your actual state on a given day is the correct input.

On primaries: always leave something. On accessories: you have more discretion.

The distinction is not conservative programming. It is accurate programming.
